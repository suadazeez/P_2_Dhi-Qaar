ABSTRACT
Abstract — The proliferation of Artificial Intelligence of Things (AIoT) devices has intensified demand for reliable, computationally efficient intrusion detection systems (IDS) capable of operating within severe resource constraints. This paper proposes a lightweight and explainable TinyML framework evaluated on the CICIoT2023 dataset. Starting from 47 traffic features, we reduced the input space to 20 statistically selected features through zero-variance elimination and correlation-based pruning. Two TinyML architectures are developed and compared: a lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP, 1,217 parameters) and a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (TinyCNN, 2,401 parameters), both converted to quantized TensorFlow Lite models. The TinyCNN achieves 99.22% accuracy and 99.21% F1-score on a balanced evaluation subset (see Section III.C), with ROC-AUC of 0.9981 and PR-AUC of 0.9985, a deployment size of 7.27 KB, and an average software-profiled inference latency of 0.0102 ms — representing a 6.2× compression over the original floating-point model. Multi-seed robustness evaluation across five independent random seed configurations confirmed highly stable predictive behavior with accuracy of 0.9922 ± 0.0004 and F1-score of 0.9921 ± 0.0004. SHAP-based explainability analysis identifies rst_count, IAT, Number, and Variance as the primary contributors to intrusion decisions, aligning with established network security domain knowledge. The proposed framework demonstrates that highly accurate, interpretable, and deployment-ready IDS models are achievable within the strict memory and computational budgets of microcontroller-class AIoT edge devices.

I. INTRODUCTION
The Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystem has expanded at an unprecedented scale, connecting billions of heterogeneous devices spanning smart homes, industrial automation, healthcare infrastructure, and critical communications networks [1]. When these devices are augmented with on-device machine learning inference — forming the Artificial Intelligence of Things (AIoT) paradigm — the security attack surface broadens considerably. Intrusion events on AIoT deployments can compromise sensitive operational data, disrupt safety-critical systems, or serve as entry points for cascading infrastructure attacks [4]. Effective intrusion detection is therefore a fundamental operational requirement rather than an optional performance enhancement.
Traditional deep learning IDS approaches achieve high detection rates but impose memory footprints, computational demands, and inference latencies that are incompatible with microcontroller-class AIoT hardware [5]. A typical ESP32-class edge node operates with 520 KB of SRAM and a clock speed under 240 MHz — parameters that exclude any model exceeding a few tens of kilobytes in quantized form. This gap has motivated TinyML: a family of compression, quantization, and architecture minimization techniques designed to deliver machine learning inference within sub-milliwatt power and sub-megabyte memory envelopes [3].
Despite growing interest, three gaps persist in TinyML-IDS research. First, most published lightweight models are not subjected to rigorous deployment-feasibility analysis — compression ratios, quantized sizes, and inference latencies are often absent or estimated rather than measured [5]. Second, explainability is frequently neglected: edge-deployed security models must provide interpretable evidence to support operator decisions and regulatory compliance [6]. Third, comparative evaluation across multiple TinyML architectures under identical experimental conditions on standardized datasets remains rare [2].
This paper addresses all three gaps with the following contributions:
(1) A systematic two-stage feature engineering pipeline reducing a 47-dimensional IoT traffic feature space to 20 statistically validated features.
(2) Two TinyML architectures — a lightweight MLP (1,217 parameters) and a TinyCNN (2,401 parameters) — both converted to quantized TensorFlow Lite models with measured deployment sizes and software-profiled inference latencies.
(3) SHAP-based explainability analysis via PermutationExplainer that identifies dominant traffic features driving intrusion decisions, providing model-agnostic transparency for security auditing.
(4) Comparative evaluation against a Random Forest baseline, demonstrating that the TinyCNN achieves detection performance within 0.48% accuracy of the baseline while occupying 7.27 KB of flash memory.
The remainder is structured as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III describes dataset and setup. Section IV presents methodology. Section V reports results. Section VI discusses findings. Section VII concludes.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Machine Learning-Based IDS for IoT
Early IoT-oriented IDS systems employed Decision Trees, Naive Bayes, and Support Vector Machines, achieving moderate detection rates but suffering from high false-positive rates and limited generalization [18]. Random Forest emerged as a strong baseline owing to ensemble decision stability and resilience to class imbalance [10]. These findings motivated our use of Random Forest as the primary performance baseline and the upper bound against which TinyML accuracy trade-offs are measured.
B. Deep Learning and Lightweight Neural Networks
Recurrent and convolutional architectures applied to network flow features demonstrated ROC-AUC values above 0.99 on benchmark datasets [4]. However, the memory requirements of such models — often hundreds of megabytes — preclude direct edge deployment. Hussain et al. and Alshehri et al. proposed compressed variants for IoT traffic classification with fewer than 5,000 parameters, showing that architectural minimalism preserves detection capability when features are carefully selected [2]. Our TinyCNN architecture builds directly on this line, extending it with TensorFlow Lite quantization and explicit latency profiling.
C. TinyML and Quantization for Edge Security
Ferrag et al. [5][16] highlighted that most published lightweight IDS models lack deployment-level evaluation. Post-training quantization via TensorFlow Lite has been shown to reduce model size by 4–8× with minimal accuracy loss for tabular classification tasks [12][21]. We applied this technique to both architectures, obtaining a 6.41× compression ratio for the MLP and approximately 6.2× for the TinyCNN, with sub-millisecond inference latencies in the TFLite runtime environment.
D. Explainable AI in Security Systems
SHAP has become the dominant post-hoc explanation framework for tabular models due to its theoretical grounding in cooperative game theory and model-agnostic applicability [11]. Several IDS studies have integrated SHAP to identify influential traffic features [14][19], but few have applied it specifically to quantized TinyML models operating under edge constraints. Our work contributes to this gap by demonstrating that PermutationExplainer-based SHAP on 20-feature lightweight architectures produces consistent, security-meaningful rankings.
E. CICIoT2023 Dataset
The CICIoT2023 dataset [9] represents one of the most comprehensive IoT attack collections available, covering 33 distinct attack categories across 47 traffic features derived from real IoT device traffic. Table I contextualizes our approach against representative works in this space.

III. DATASET AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Dataset
We used the CICIoT2023 dataset [9], which contains 5,491,971 network traffic samples collected from real IoT devices under controlled attack and benign conditions. The original dataset includes 47 statistical and protocol-derived features covering packet-level statistics, inter-arrival timing, flag counts, and protocol indicators, alongside multi-class attack labels spanning 33 categories including DDoS, DoS, reconnaissance, and brute-force variants.
B. Binary Formulation
We reformulated the classification task as binary intrusion detection — benign versus attack. Multi-class categorization increases model parameter counts and decision complexity substantially. For edge-constrained environments where the primary operational requirement is reliable attack detection with minimal latency, binary formulation is the appropriate design choice. We acknowledge this as a deliberate constraint and discuss its implications in Section VI.
C. Balanced Subset Construction and Evaluation Scope
The original dataset exhibits severe class imbalance. To stabilize training and enable symmetric evaluation, we constructed a balanced subset of 50,000 benign and 50,000 attack samples. We explicitly acknowledge that the metrics reported throughout this paper represent performance under controlled, idealized conditions — an upper bound relative to real-world deployment. In real-world AIoT traffic distributions, where benign flows substantially outnumber attack flows, the model would require additional mitigation strategies such as class-weighted training or adaptive decision thresholding to preserve recall above 95% for the minority attack class. ROC-AUC and PR-AUC metrics are reported precisely because they are less susceptible to class distribution effects and better reflect the model's discriminative capability under varying operating conditions.
D. Train-Test Split and Data Leakage Prevention
The dataset was divided using stratified train-test splitting preserving the 50/50 class ratio: 80% for training (80,000 samples) and 20% for testing (20,000 samples), with a fixed random seed of 42 for full reproducibility. Feature normalization was applied using StandardScaler with scaling parameters learned exclusively from the training subset and subsequently applied to the test subset. Feature selection was performed only on training data. These precautions prevent data leakage and ensure that all reported test-set metrics reflect genuinely held-out performance.
E. Feature Engineering
Starting from 47 features, we first removed 12 features exhibiting zero or near-zero variance — features whose statistical spread across the balanced subset was insufficient to support discriminative learning. In a second pass, we removed additional features based on pairwise Pearson correlation analysis, eliminating those with coefficients exceeding 0.95 against already-retained features. This two-stage process yielded a final compact feature set of 20 variables. Table II summarizes dataset configuration and Table III details the removed features.

IV. METHODOLOGY
A. Lightweight MLP Architecture
The primary TinyML model is a multilayer perceptron designed under strict parameter minimization constraints. The architecture processes 20 input features through Dense(32, ReLU) → Dropout(0.2) → Dense(16, ReLU) → Dropout(0.2) → Sigmoid output. Total parameters: 1,217. The model was trained using Adam (lr=0.001, β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999), binary cross-entropy loss, 10 epochs, batch size 256, 20% validation split.
 
Fig. 1. Proposed Lightweight MLP TinyML IDS Architecture.
B. Proposed TinyCNN Architecture
To evaluate whether convolutional feature extraction improves performance within the same TinyML parameter budget, we designed a one-dimensional convolutional variant. The TinyCNN accepts reshaped input tensors of dimension (20,1) and applies Conv1D(16 filters, kernel=3, ReLU) → MaxPooling1D(pool=2) → Flatten → Dense(16, ReLU) → Dropout(0.2) → Sigmoid output. Total parameters: 2,401 (64 convolutional, 2,320 dense, 17 output). Training configuration was identical to the MLP over 20 epochs.
The Conv1D layer captures local correlations across adjacent features in the ordered feature vector — for instance, relationships between packet size statistics and timing features that are spatially proximate after preprocessing. This structured inductive bias is the primary reason for TinyCNN's marginal but consistent performance advantage over the fully-connected MLP.
 
Fig. 2. Proposed TinyCNN TinyML IDS Architecture.
C. Post-Training Quantization
Both models were converted to TensorFlow Lite format using post-training dynamic range quantization, mapping 32-bit floating-point weights and activations to 8-bit integer representations. This reduces model binary size by approximately 4–8× and enables deployment on hardware without FPU support. One hundred representative training samples were provided as a calibration dataset during conversion to minimize quantization-induced accuracy loss. Quantized sizes: MLP 6.80 KB (6.41× compression), TinyCNN 7.27 KB (~6.2× compression).
D. Runtime Profiling
Inference latency was measured using the TensorFlow Lite Python Interpreter, executing 1,000 inference cycles per model and averaging the results. This environment does not replicate the timing characteristics of physical microcontrollers. We explicitly note this as a limitation: hardware-level latency on constrained devices will differ and should be measured in future physical deployment studies. The software measurements provide a consistent, reproducible baseline for comparative analysis.
E. SHAP Explainability
We applied PermutationExplainer from the SHAP library to the trained MLP model, using 100 randomly selected training instances as the background reference set. PermutationExplainer is model-agnostic and requires no access to internal model gradients or architecture details, making it well-suited for TinyML models where internal representations are deliberately minimized. The low feature dimensionality (M=20) makes the permutation approach computationally tractable without approximation loss. The resulting SHAP summary ranks all 20 features by mean absolute contribution, disaggregated by feature value using a color axis from low (blue) to high (red) values.

V. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
A. Detection Performance
Table IV presents classification metrics for all three models on the 20,000-sample test set. The Random Forest baseline achieved 99.69% accuracy and 99.68% F1-score — a strong upper bound given its unconstrained footprint. The lightweight MLP achieved 99.17% accuracy and 99.16% F1-score, a 0.52 percentage point degradation relative to the baseline. The TinyCNN achieved a mean accuracy of 99.22% and mean F1-score of 99.21% across five independent runs, slightly outperforming the MLP and narrowing the gap with Random Forest to 0.47 percentage points. In probabilistic discrimination, the TinyCNN achieved ROC-AUC of 0.9981 and PR-AUC of 0.9985, confirming near-perfect separation capability despite extreme parameter compactness.

B. Deployment Efficiency
Table V summarizes runtime and resource metrics. The MLP achieved average inference latency of 0.0163 ms and the TinyCNN 0.0102 ms — a 37% reduction attributable to pooling-induced dimensionality reduction in the convolutional branch. Both quantized model sizes (6.80 KB and 7.27 KB) are well within the flash capacity of ESP32-class devices (typically 4 MB) and represent a fraction of the SRAM required for inference buffers. At these latencies, both models are capable of analyzing tens of thousands of traffic samples per second, which is sufficient for real-time IDS operation on typical IoT edge nodes.

The MLP training curve (Fig. 5) shows rapid initial learning in the first two epochs, followed by stable convergence through epoch 10 with no evidence of overfitting. The small gap between training and validation accuracy — with validation marginally ahead in early epochs — is normal behavior for dropout-regularized networks, where dropout is active during training but not validation. The TinyCNN (Fig. 6) exhibits a similar profile across 20 epochs, with curves converging to within 0.01% by epoch 10 and remaining stable thereafter, indicating that both architectures generalize well without requiring early stopping.

D. SHAP Feature Importance
The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 7) reveals that rst_count, IAT, Number, and Variance are the four most influential features, collectively accounting for the largest magnitude SHAP contributions. rst_count — the count of TCP reset flags in a flow — is a well-established indicator of port scanning and connection teardown attacks. IAT (inter-arrival time) captures temporal irregularities characteristic of DoS and flooding attacks. Number (packet count per flow) and Variance (statistical spread of packet sizes) jointly reflect behavioral anomalies. The alignment between SHAP rankings and established network security domain knowledge validates that the feature selection pipeline retained semantically meaningful variables rather than statistical artifacts.

 
Fig. 7. SHAP Summary Plot — Feature contributions to intrusion detection decisions. Top features (rst_count, IAT, Number, Variance) align with known attack behavioral signatures.
E. Multi-Seed Robustness Evaluation
To further assess experimental stability, the TinyCNN architecture was evaluated across five independent random seed configurations (seeds: 42, 123, 456, 789, 999), with full retraining from scratch for each run. The results demonstrated highly stable predictive behavior with minimal statistical deviation: accuracy of 0.9922 ± 0.0004 and F1-score of 0.9921 ± 0.0004, as reported in Table IV. The standard deviation across seeds remained below 0.0011 for all metrics, confirming that the proposed lightweight architecture maintains consistent detection capability under varying initialization conditions and is not sensitive to the specific random seed used during training.

VI. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
A. Why TinyCNN Outperforms MLP
The 0.04% accuracy gain from TinyCNN over MLP is small in absolute terms but consistent. The Conv1D layer captures local correlations across adjacent features — attacks such as SYN floods manifest as simultaneous anomalies in packet count, size variance, and flag counts that are spatially proximate in the feature vector. A convolution with kernel size 3 learns these joint patterns without increasing parameter count quadratically, as would be required by additional dense layers. This validates that structural priors (locality in feature space) are beneficial for lightweight models on structured network traffic data [15].
B. Explainability in Ultra-Lightweight Models
Despite 2,401 parameters, TinyCNN admits meaningful SHAP explanations. The permutation approach succeeds because low feature dimensionality (M=20) keeps the sampling complexity tractable, and model simplicity reduces approximation variance [11]. Critically, SHAP rankings aligned with Random Forest feature importance and domain security knowledge — increasing trust for security analysts and supporting the argument that the model has learned generalizable patterns rather than dataset-specific correlations [14].
C. Practical Deployment Considerations
Both quantized models fit comfortably within the flash and SRAM budgets of ESP32-class and ARM Cortex-M-class microcontrollers. The software-profiled latency figures (0.0102–0.0163 ms) represent a lower bound; actual on-device latency will be higher due to memory access patterns, interrupt overhead, and absence of hardware floating-point units. We treat the current software profiling as a preliminary feasibility indicator. Physical deployment validation on target hardware remains the most important next experimental step, and we explicitly flag it as such rather than extrapolating software results to hardware claims.
D. Limitations
Despite the promising experimental results, several limitations constrain the scope of our conclusions. First, although the TinyCNN architecture demonstrated highly stable predictive performance across five independent random seed configurations, the evaluation remains limited to the CICIoT2023 dataset under a binary intrusion detection formulation. Additional cross-dataset validation on benchmarks such as BoT-IoT or MQTT-IoT-IDS remains necessary to assess generalization under heterogeneous AIoT traffic conditions. Second, the experimental evaluation relied on a balanced subset extracted from the original CICIoT2023 dataset. While this strategy enabled controlled comparative analysis, real-world AIoT deployments exhibit substantially different attack prevalence rates and evolving traffic distributions that were not addressed in this study. Third, runtime profiling was conducted within the TensorFlow Lite software environment rather than on physical microcontroller hardware. The reported latency measurements should therefore be interpreted as feasibility-oriented software benchmarks rather than hardware-native embedded measurements. Fourth, the present study focused on binary intrusion detection; adversarial robustness evaluation, energy consumption profiling, and hardware-level deployment optimization remain important directions for future investigation.

VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a lightweight and explainable TinyML framework for intrusion detection in resource-constrained AIoT edge environments. We reduced a 47-feature IoT traffic dataset to a compact 20-feature representation, trained and quantized two TinyML architectures — a lightweight MLP and a TinyCNN — and integrated SHAP-based explainability to provide transparent, auditable intrusion decisions.
The TinyCNN achieved a mean accuracy of 99.22% and F1-score of 99.21% across five independent random seed configurations (σ < 0.0011 for all metrics), with ROC-AUC of 0.9981, a quantized deployment size of 7.27 KB, and an average software-profiled inference latency of 0.0102 ms, demonstrating that near-baseline intrusion detection capability is achievable within the severe constraints of microcontroller-class AIoT hardware. The SHAP analysis confirmed that model decisions are grounded in semantically meaningful traffic features aligned with established network security knowledge.
Future work will focus on: (1) physical deployment and latency profiling on ESP32 and ARM Cortex-M hardware under realistic IoT traffic; (2) extension to multi-class attack categorization using TinyML architectures with class-specific calibration; (3) evaluation on additional IoT-specific benchmarks such as BoT-IoT and MQTT-IoT-IDS to assess cross-dataset generalization; and (4) adversarial robustness assessment and energy consumption profiling under realistic edge operating conditions.
